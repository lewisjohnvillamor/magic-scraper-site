Two tables, one page, different column counts. Automatic detection picks the bigger one — the 14-row international table — so if you want the 8-row domestic one you have to ask for it. That is what the Another table control is for.

About this practice page

Shipping rates in two tables

Carriers, couriers and shops publish delivery charges like this: one table for home, one for abroad, and they are never the same shape. If you are comparing carriers or building a shipping calculator, you usually need both — and a scraper that takes “the table on the page” gives you one.

Which table do you get?

The domestic table has 8 zones and 5 columns (Zone, Covers, Transit, Standard, Trade). The international one has 14 countries and 6 (Country, Code, Transit, Flat rate, Free over, Duties). Automatic detection picks the bigger one, the international table, because it has more rows. That is the right guess more often than not, and wrong whenever you came for the other.

Getting each one

In Magic Scraper, open it and you have the 14 international rows. For the domestic zones, open Wrong list? and choose Another table; it moves to the next candidate on the page and outlines it, so you can see which you have before exporting. In Python both come back at once, in page order:

import pandas as pd
domestic, international = pd.read_html("https://magicscraper.app/demo/rates")
domestic.shape, international.shape   # (8, 5) (14, 6)

Do not try to stack them into one sheet: the columns mean different things. Keep two tabs, or add a Region column to each before joining.

Reading the values

Several cells are words, not numbers: Free in the trade column for zones 1 to 3, and an em dash under Free over for the countries where there is no free threshold. Decide what they mean before summing — Free is 0; the dash is “never”, which is not 0. The Duties column is a code: DDP means duties are paid by the sender, DAP that they are collected on delivery.

A correct result

8 rows by 5 columns, or 14 rows by 6. Zone 1, Greater Manchester, is $6.50 standard; Japan is the most expensive at $39.00 flat.
